01 What Is Information Security?

The official definition and the CIA Triad

Information Security (InfoSec) deals with several different "trust" aspects of information and how to protect them. The exact definition, straight from the U.S. Government's Committee on National Security Systems, is worth memorizing:

CNSS (2010) definition: "The protection of information and information systems from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction in order to provide confidentiality, integrity, and availability."

Notice the definition ends by naming exactly three goals — this is the famous CIA Triad, the single most testable idea in this whole chapter:

Element What it actually means
Confidentiality Only people who are supposed to see the data can see it
Integrity The data hasn't been altered, whether by accident or on purpose
Availability (Recoverability) The data/system is there and working when authorized people need it — and can be recovered if something goes wrong
Think of it like a bank vault: Confidentiality is the locked door (only authorized people get in). Integrity is a tamper-proof seal on the money bags (no one quietly swapped the contents). Availability is the vault actually opening on schedule for the teller who legitimately needs cash for a customer — a vault that's "secure" but permanently jammed shut has failed just as badly as one that was robbed.

Don't forget: security isn't only digital

  • InfoSec includes Physical Security (locked doors, guarded server rooms) just as much as electronic/cyber security
  • The slide's diagram frames it as a layered system: Information sits at the center (C/I/A), surrounded by Hardware/Software/Communications, then wrapped in Products (Physical Security), People (Personnel Security), and Procedures (Organizational Security)
Common exam trap: Don't answer "confidentiality" when a question is really describing data being corrupted — that's integrity. And don't answer "confidentiality" for a system being knocked offline — that's availability. Match the scenario to the exact letter of the triad it violates.

02 Malware — The Umbrella Term

One word that covers almost everything else in this chapter

Definition: "Malicious" + "software" = malware — hostile, intrusive, or annoying software or program code. It includes viruses, worms, trojan horses, bots, spyware, adware, and more.
Exam-critical distinction: Whether something counts as malware depends on the intent of the creator, not on any specific technical feature. The exact same piece of tracking code could be "legitimate analytics" in one context and "malware" in another — what makes it malware is that it's doing something hostile, intrusive, or unwanted from the user's perspective.

03 The Named Threats

Bots, adware, spyware, spam, and phishing — know what separates each one

Term What it is Key detail to remember
Internet bot An automated application controlled by a software agent that interacts with services meant for people A botnet is a network of "zombie" computers doing automated tasks like spamming — bots aren't always malicious (e.g. a game bot), but botnets usually are
Adware Software that automatically plays/displays/downloads ads on your computer Often bundled into free software as a way to recover development costs through ad revenue — the intent is commercial, not necessarily malicious
Spyware Software that intercepts or partially controls a computer without informed consent Watches what you do and sends that info elsewhere — can log visited sites, capture typed passwords/credit card numbers, and trigger pop-up ads. Many legitimate companies bundle spyware-like tracking as a form of adware
Spam Abuse of electronic messaging to send unsolicited, undesired bulk messages Email is the most recognized form, but it also spreads through IM, Usenet, search engines, blogs, and mobile messaging
Phishing Social engineering used to acquire sensitive data (passwords, credit card info) by pretending to be a trustworthy party Typically delivered via email or instant message — the classic example is a fake "your bank" email with a link to a look-alike site
The classic phishing tell: the slide's TrustedBank example email looks legitimate, but the link actually points to a "bad" IP address rather than the bank's real domain. Always check where a link actually goes, not just what text is displayed.
Exam trap — bots vs. botnets vs. spyware vs. adware: These four get mixed up constantly. The quick test: does it watch and report on you (spyware)? Does it just show ads (adware)? Does it act autonomously doing tasks (bot)? Is it a whole network of compromised machines (botnet)? Match the behavior described in the question to exactly one of these.

04 Ransomware

The threat that attacks Availability directly

Ransomware encrypts a victim's data and demands payment (a "ransom") to restore access. The slide statistics make three points worth remembering:

Numbers worth remembering

  • Government organizations were the most-affected sector in North America (15.4% of reported ransom attacks), followed by manufacturing and construction
  • Reporting rates vary enormously by country — from 88% of organizations in Saudi Arabia down to 38% in Japan
  • 99% of victims whose data was encrypted got some data back — but the #1 method of recovery was backups, not paying the ransom
  • Only 46% of victims actually paid the ransom, and of those, just 4% got all their data back (61% was restored on average)
Exam-relevant takeaway: Paying the ransom is neither guaranteed nor the primary way organizations actually recover — good backups matter more. This ties directly back to the Availability leg of the CIA Triad: ransomware's whole attack is making your own data unavailable to you.

05 Keystroke Logging

A tool that's dual-use — legitimate and malicious

Keystroke logging (keylogging) captures a user's keystrokes. It originated as a diagnostic tool in software development, but the same capability is exactly what makes it dangerous in the wrong hands.

Legitimate uses Malicious uses
Finding sources of error in software during development Capturing passwords or encryption keys, bypassing other security measures entirely
Measuring employee productivity on clerical tasks Espionage / unauthorized surveillance of a user
Law enforcement (with proper authorization) Widely available online, so anyone can repurpose the same tool

Two delivery mechanisms

  • Hardware keyloggers — three types: an inline device attached to the keyboard cable, a device installed inside a standard keyboard, or a keyboard with the logger built in from the factory
  • Software keyloggers — trivial to write, and can be distributed as malware (virus, trojan, etc.) just like any other program
Exam trap: Don't assume keylogging is inherently "malware" — the technology itself is neutral (used legitimately in QA and productivity measurement). Just like the malware definition in Section 2, what makes a specific keylogger malicious is how and why it was deployed, not the mechanism itself.

06 Why Is It Important?

The threat landscape shifted, and it costs real money

The landscape shift (memorize this framing)

  • Traditional malware threats hit an apparent wall around 2005
  • New threats — bots, spam, phishing — stepped into that void instead of threats disappearing

How each threat maps back onto the CIA Triad:

Triad element How it gets attacked
Confidentiality Unauthorized access via malware/spyware limits our ability to protect data confidentiality
Integrity Malicious programs can alter data values, destroying data integrity
Availability Denial of Service (DoS) attacks can shut down a server/network, making the system unavailable
Numbers cited in the slides — plausible exam fill-in-the-blanks:
  • Over eight million phishing attempts per day, on average, during the latter half of 2005 (Symantec)
  • Spam cost U.S. organizations more than $10 billion in 2004 (California legislature), including lost productivity and the extra equipment/software/manpower needed to fight it

Regulatory drivers — why this isn't optional for many organizations:

Regulation What it covers
HIPAA Protection of electronic personal identifiable health information
Sarbanes-Oxley Act Federal securities law focused on data accuracy and integrity
PCI Security Payment Card Industry security measures for handling card data

Growing/emerging concerns named in the slides

  • Liability for damage caused by botnets
  • Loss of confidential corporate information (financials, personnel data)
  • Electronic blackmail

07 What Can We Do?

Three named strategies — assessment, application, awareness

  • Security Assessment — identify areas of risk, identify potential for security breaches/collapses, identify concrete steps to mitigate them
  • Security Application — apply expert knowledge (train staff, hire experts, or both); use a multi-layered approach since there is no single solution; put real policies and procedures in place
  • Security Awareness — not just for "the geeks": training at all levels of the organization (external and/or internal), continuing education (not a one-time event), and making it part of the organization's culture
    • "Multi-layered approach" explained: think of airport security — there's no single checkpoint that catches everything. ID check, bag scan, metal detector, and behavioral observation each catch different things. InfoSec works the same way: firewalls, training, patching, and policy all catch different failure modes, and none of them alone is sufficient.

    One-Page Recap

    • InfoSec's job: protect information/systems from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction — in order to provide the CIA Triad: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability.
    • Security is layered: physical security (Products), people (Personnel/Procedures), and electronic/cyber security all matter together.
    • Malware is defined by the creator's intent, not a specific feature — it's the umbrella term over viruses, worms, trojans, bots, spyware, and adware.
    • Threat vocabulary: bots/botnets (automated tasks at scale), adware (unwanted ads), spyware (covert monitoring), spam (unsolicited bulk messaging), phishing (social-engineered credential theft).
    • Ransomware attacks Availability directly; backups — not paying the ransom — are the #1 real-world recovery method.
    • Keylogging is dual-use: legitimate in QA/productivity measurement and law enforcement, but the exact same mechanism enables password/key theft when used maliciously.
    • Why it matters: real financial cost (billions in spam-related losses alone), regulatory pressure (HIPAA, Sarbanes-Oxley, PCI), and growing risks like botnet liability and electronic blackmail.
    • Response strategy: Assessment → Application (multi-layered, policy-backed) → Awareness (ongoing, cultural, for everyone).
    • Key takeaway line from the slides: "Failure to Secure is an Opportunity to Fail."
